Teaching notes

Tabs 1 and 2 are built to be projected and talked through. Tab 3 works better on student devices, in pairs, before they start Part 5.

Where this sits in the Day 11 guide

  • Part 2 → Part 3. The second question in tab 3 is the exact jump your guide makes: two loops stacked in a row versus one loop inside another. Students who miss this think nesting is just “more loops.”
  • Part 3. Tab 2 opens on the pinwheel from your guide — same numbers, range(6), range(4), setMove("F", 8, 50), 60° rotate.
  • Part 4. The indent bars in the code panel light up to show which loops own the current line, and the sentence under the code says it in words. The 4-spaces-vs-8-spaces question in tab 3 is the same idea as a prediction.
  • Part 5. The preset buttons are the Bronze list, and the outer sliders reach 36 for the Gold spirograph.

The three errors this targets

  • Adding instead of multiplying. Students read range(5) around range(3) as 8 actions. The running tally in tab 1 ticks past 8 while they watch.
  • Thinking the inner loop runs once for the whole program. Each pass of the outer loop draws in a different marker color, so “the square got drawn six times” is something they see rather than something you tell them.
  • Indentation as decoration. Most pairs will mis-indent the 60° turn at least once. Showing them the broken version on purpose, before it happens by accident, turns it from a failure into a prediction they got right.

Lines that work when a pair is stuck

  • “Put your finger on the last line of the inner loop. Python can't get past your finger until the inner loop is done. How many times does it hit your finger?”
  • “Which loop draws a square? Which loop draws lots of squares?”
  • “Read it inside out: the 8-space lines are what actually happens, the 4-space loop makes a shape, the no-indent loop makes a pattern.”
  • Unplugged for the ones who need their body involved: outer loop = rows of desks, inner loop = students in the row. Walk it. You don't start row 2 until row 1 is finished.

The outer turn that closes a pattern back to the start is always 360 ÷ outer repeats. Worth putting on the board — it's the one piece of arithmetic in Part 5 that pairs tend to guess at.

Two things to do live

  • Set the outer turn to 0° and run it. Six squares, all stacked in the same spot. Anyone who thinks the inner loop only runs once has to explain where the extra ink came from.
  • Step one line at a time through the first two passes with the class calling out the counters before you press. Then let it play.

On the real robot

A nested pattern drawn with the marker holder will drift — six squares won't land perfectly on top of each other. That's not a bug to fix before moving on. “Your code is right and your drawing is crooked” is the Day 8 test-and-iterate habit showing up again, and it's a genuinely useful thing for a seventh grader to sit with.

For pairs who need the block version first, the structure maps directly: a Repeat block inside a Repeat block, with the 60° turn in the outer Repeat but below the inner one.
